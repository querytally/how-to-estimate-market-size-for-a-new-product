# How to Estimate Market Size for a New Product

To estimate the market size of a new product, multiply four numbers: the buyers you can reach, the share of them who
look for a product like yours in a year, the price, and how many times a year each one buys. Then do the sum a second
way, from a larger published total down, and trust the estimate only when the two answers land close together.

## The worksheet

Copy these four lines and fill one column per product idea. Write a note next to each input saying where the number
came from. An input with no note is a guess, and the guide treats it as one.

| line | what goes in it | the question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Buyers | people or businesses who have the problem, in the places you will sell | who could buy at all? |
| 2. Share who search | the part of line 1 that goes looking for a fix in a year | who is in the market now? |
| 3. Price | what one purchase costs the buyer, after typical discounts | what is one sale worth? |
| 4. Frequency | purchases per buyer per year (0.5 means one every two years) | how often does the sale repeat? |
| **Market size** | line 1 × line 2 × line 3 × line 4 | what the market spends a year |

The result is your serviceable market: the buyers you can actually reach, in the places you serve. It is not the share
you will win. That comes next.

## A worked example

Say a small company plans a chew-proof bed for large dogs, sold online in the US. The numbers below are round and made
up; they only show the sum.

| line | input | running total |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Buyers | 2,000,000 households with a large dog that chews | 2,000,000 |
| 2. Share who search | 5 % shop for a sturdier bed in a given year | 100,000 |
| 3. Price | 120 dollars a bed | 12,000,000 dollars |
| 4. Frequency | 0.5 (a bed lasts about two years) | **6,000,000 dollars a year** |

Now the second sum, from the top. Take a published figure for what US owners spend on dog beds in a year, then the
part that goes to beds for large dogs, then the part sold at a premium price. If that lands near 6 million, the
estimate holds. If it lands at 40 million or at 1 million, one input on the worksheet is wrong. Find it before you
order stock.

Last, the obtainable share: what one new brand can win in its first year or two. A plan built on 2 % of the
serviceable market (about 120,000 dollars a year here) is honest. A plan built on the whole 6 million is not.

## Line 2 is the one to test

Lines 1, 3 and 4 can usually be checked against something written down. Line 2 is the guess, and it moves the answer
more than any other line. Two ways to firm it up:

- **Count who asks.** People in the market look for answers before they buy: they ask what the product costs, which
  kind lasts, what to do when the old one fails. How many people ask, each month, is a direct reading of line 2.
- **Ask a sample.** Ten short conversations with people from line 1 ("when did you last shop for this?") will not
  give a precise share, but they will tell you whether 5 % is near 1 % or near 20 %.

Sizing a market in searches works the same way at a bigger scale. One whole market, freight factoring, came to about
309,080 US searches a month, and 272 of its 1,569 searches came from people who would never buy. A count that leaves
the wrong audience in is a market you will never collect. [Querytally](https://querytally.com/market-analysis/market-size/)
explains how to read an estimate like that, layer by layer.

## Three estimation mistakes

**1. Leaving frequency at 1.** Many first worksheets assume one purchase a year by default. A product bought every
three years is a third of that market; a refill bought monthly is twelve times it. Write the real rhythm of the
purchase.

**2. Borrowing a share from another product.** A share that held for phone cases says nothing about dog beds. A share
copied from a different product, buyer or country makes line 2 a number with no source, and the whole estimate rests
on it.

**3. Stopping after one direction.** A bottom-up sum feels precise because every line is small and specific. Without
the top-down sum next to it, there is nothing to catch a wrong input. Two answers that agree are worth more than one
detailed answer.

## Before you rely on the number

- Each of the four lines has a note saying where it came from.
- The estimate names its country and its period (US, per year).
- The top-down sum lands within a factor of two or so of the bottom-up one.
- You can say who the first hundred buyers are and where they look.

If all four hold, the number is ready to plan with.
